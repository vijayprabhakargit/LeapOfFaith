## What was covered

- The necessity of distortion: sphere versus plane, Gauss's intrinsic-curvature argument, the orange-peel demonstration.
- The four map properties — area, shape, distance, direction — and why no projection preserves all four; Tissot's indicatrix as the audit tool.
- Scale in three forms: statement, representative fraction (unit-free) and graphical (survives reproduction); the 1:50,000 worked arithmetic (1 cm = 500 m).
- Large versus small scale explained via the size of the fraction, with the Indian ladder from cadastral 1:4,000 to world 1:50,000,000.
- The three projection families — cylindrical/Mercator (1569, conformal, rhumb lines straight, area inflated at high latitudes), conic (mid-latitude east–west extents, meridians converging on an apex), azimuthal (true direction from the centre, polar maps, UN emblem); plus compromise projections (Robinson, Winkel Tripel from 1998).
- Topographic sheets and conventional signs; contour rules (no crossing, spacing = slope, rings = hill, hachures = depression, V points upstream); gradient as vertical interval divided by horizontal equivalent; spot heights and trigonometrical stations.
- Remote sensing (passive versus active sensors; four resolutions; IRS from 1988, Cartosat, RISAT, Bhuvan) versus GIS (layering and overlay analysis), with GNSS as the positioning leg.
- The closing argument that every map is an argument — projection, scale, classification and omission as both technical and political choices.
